How the numbers are calculated

Friction loss uses the Darcy-Weisbach equation with the friction factor from the Colebrook equation, as given in the ASHRAE Handbook, Fundamentals, duct design chapter. It assumes standard air at 0.075 lb/ft3, kinematic viscosity 1.62e-4 ft2/s and galvanized steel with a roughness of 0.0003 ft (0.09 mm), the same basis as most printed ductulators. The round diameter is found by solving that equation for the friction rate you enter.

Rectangular sizes use the Huebscher equivalent diameter, De = 1.30 (a b)^0.625 / (a + b)^0.25, which gives the round duct with the same friction loss at the same airflow. Widths are rounded up to the next 2 inches (50 mm), so the real friction is at or below your target. A rectangular duct with an aspect ratio over 4 to 1 costs more and is noisier.

Common design values

Many residential and light commercial designs use a friction rate of 0.08 to 0.1 in.wg per 100 ft and keep supply branches near 600 to 900 fpm and mains below about 1,200 to 1,500 fpm. Your code, equipment data and project specification come first.

How to size a duct run, step by step

  1. Get the airflow for each section. Take CFM per room or outlet from your load calculation or equipment data. A common rule of thumb for cooling is about 400 CFM per ton, but a real room-by-room calculation replaces it. A branch carries only what its outlets need. The main carries the sum of its branches.
  2. Pick a friction rate for the whole system. Equal friction keeps one rate for every section, so the fan sees a balanced system. Start with 0.08 to 0.1 in.wg per 100 ft. Use the maximum velocity option where noise matters, for example a branch to a bedroom.
  3. Read the round diameter and go to the next stock size. The calculator shows both the exact value and the stock size, with the velocity and friction at each.
  4. Convert to rectangular if the space is tight. Choose the height the ceiling or joist bay allows, then take the width from the table the calculator prints. Keep the aspect ratio under 4 to 1 where you can.
  5. Add fittings and the longest run. The friction rate only covers straight duct. Total the equivalent length of elbows, transitions and grilles on the longest run before you check the fan.

Round equivalent of a rectangular duct

Equivalent diameter De = 1.30 (a b)^0.625 / (a + b)^0.25 for common rectangular sizes in inches. Two ducts with the same De lose the same pressure per foot at the same CFM. The equivalent round is not the diameter of equal area: a 12 by 12 duct (144 square inches) has an equivalent round of 13.1 in, which has 135 square inches.

What this calculator leaves out

  • Air density. The model uses standard air at 0.075 lb/ft3. At high elevation or high temperature the air is thinner and the friction per foot changes, so correct for density on those jobs.
  • Duct material. Roughness 0.0003 ft is galvanized steel. Flexible duct, fiberglass duct board and spiral duct have different roughness and different friction.
  • Fittings and dynamic losses. Elbows, tees, transitions, dampers and grilles add loss that depends on the fitting. Use the manufacturer or SMACNA loss coefficients.
  • Manual D and code. The result is a sizing aid, not a design package.

Questions

What is a ductulator?

A ductulator is the slide-rule style duct sizing tool, a printed version of the friction chart. This page does the same job: pick CFM and friction rate or velocity, read off the round size, then look up the rectangular equivalent.

Should I size by friction rate or by velocity?

The equal friction method is the usual starting point, because it keeps the system roughly balanced. Use the velocity option when noise limits the duct, for example near occupied rooms.

Does this include fittings and flex duct?

No. The result is for straight, smooth galvanized duct. Fittings add pressure loss, and flexible duct has a higher friction than metal. Add allowances for both.

How many CFM does a duct carry?

It depends on the friction rate and the duct size, not on the duct alone. At 0.1 in.wg per 100 ft a 10 in round duct carries about 400 CFM (the table above gives 9.7 in exact, so 10 in has a little room), a 14 in round carries about 1,000 CFM and an 18 in round about 2,000 CFM.

How do I convert a round duct to a rectangular duct?

Find the equivalent round diameter for the round size, pick the height your space allows, then solve the Huebscher equation for the width. The calculator prints the widths for heights from 6 to 30 in. Or read the rectangular table above for common sizes.

Is a 4 to 1 aspect ratio a hard limit?

No, it is a common guideline. Tall narrow ducts have more surface per unit of area, so they cost more and are noisier. The calculator marks anything above 4 to 1 as high.

What velocity is too high?

It depends on where the duct is. Many residential designs keep branches near 600 to 900 fpm and mains below about 1,200 to 1,500 fpm. The calculator warns above 1,500 fpm. Your project specification sets the real limit.

Does anything get sent anywhere?

No. The calculation runs in your browser and nothing is uploaded or stored.

This is a design aid, not a substitute for a full load and duct design. Check results against your code, manufacturer data and ACCA or SMACNA procedures.
